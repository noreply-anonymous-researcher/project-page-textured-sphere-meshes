Abstract

Sphere meshes have been introduced as geometric proxies that maintain structural details better than polygon meshes under drastic simplification. Despite this potential, they have not yet been used as high-quality level-of-detail (LOD) models. One possible explanation is the lack of techniques for generating high-quality, texture-mapped sphere meshes and rendering them efficiently. In this work, we address this gap with a complete pipeline for generating and rendering high-quality sphere meshes guided by visual error. First, we introduce a geometry optimization module that jointly adapts topology and primitive placement to improve geometric approximation. Second, we derive texture coordinates for sphere meshes by ray casting against the original polygonal mesh, pack the resulting UV maps into a compact texture atlas, and apply appearance-driven texture optimization to remove artifacts. Third, we present efficient rendering approaches using either rasterization or raytracing. We evaluate our method by comparing textured sphere meshes with conventional polygonal level-of-detail meshes. Our results show that sphere meshes achieve better visual quality under comparable primitive and rendering budgets, especially for structurally complex meshes.

Sphere Meshes in a Nutshell

A sphere mesh is a set of spheres connected by a medial mesh. Interpolating the spheres along its edges and faces gives the final shape: every vertex is a sphere, every edge a rounded truncated cone, and every face a rounded triangular prism. Tubular structures such as legs, handles or wings need only a few primitives, where a triangle mesh at the same budget loses them.

For rendering, we split every sphere mesh into three types of primitives:

  • Spheres: used as they are.
  • Lateral cone surfaces: a cone is two spheres plus its lateral surface.
  • Triangles: a prism is three spheres, three cone surfaces and two triangles.

Method

1

Appearance-driven geometry optimization

Starting from a Structural-MAT collapse history, a loss-guided beam search over split and collapse operations explores alternative cuts and keeps the top-k candidates per layer. This lets it move the sphere budget into structurally complex regions and avoid the local minima of greedy edge collapses. With the selected topology fixed, gradient-based refinement then adjusts sphere centers and radii. Both stages minimize the same multi-view silhouette, depth and normal loss ℒgeom between the rendered sphere mesh and the input mesh.

2

Texture atlas generation and optimization

Every primitive gets its own bijective parameterization: a cube map for spheres, an unrolled envelope for cones and a rigid transform for triangles. Occluded regions are trimmed from the patches before they are shelf-packed into a texture atlas. The atlas is initialized by raycasting against the input mesh along the surface normal and then refined by differentiable texture optimization. For fixed visibility this is a convex weighted least-squares problem, where the weights down-weight grazing angles and geometric mismatches.

3

Efficient rendering

Billboard raycasting runs in a standard rasterizer. Mesh shaders emit tight screen-facing billboards for spheres and cones, and the analytic ray–primitive intersection gives pixel-exact outlines, depth and normals. Conservative depth keeps early-Z culling active, and a visibility buffer limits overdraw. Alternatively, a raytracing pipeline uses custom intersection shaders or the hardware-accelerated linear swept sphere (LSS) primitives.
